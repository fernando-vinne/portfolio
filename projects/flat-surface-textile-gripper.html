Flat-Surface Textile Gripper

Development of a robotic gripper for picking deformable textiles from flat surfaces. The project turned a broad handling problem into a working gripper through rapid prototyping, silicone casting, and iterative testing, up to integration in deployed robotic prototype systems.

Context Industrial robotics project

Overview

The goal of this project was to develop a gripper that could reliably pick textile pieces from flat surfaces. This is difficult because textiles are deformable, irregular, and often hard to separate from the surface, especially when the available gripping point is close to an edge or corner.

I took the gripper from early concepts to working hardware: building and testing physical prototypes, analysing failure cases such as corners and edges, and refining the design until it was reliable enough to be integrated into robotic laundry-sorting prototypes running in a real industrial environment.

My contribution

I was responsible for translating the concepts into physical prototypes, testing them, understanding the failure modes, and improving the design toward a robust, repeatable gripper.

  • Tested existing and early-stage solutions to understand the real gripping problem.
  • Developed and evaluated different mechanical concepts for flat-surface textile pickup.
  • Designed and iterated prototypes using CAD, 3D printing, silicone casting, and assembly testing.
  • Iterated the gripper design based on test results to improve grip reliability.
  • Adapted the concept for integration into larger robotic laundry-sorting prototypes.
  • Improved part quality and repeatability, including the silicone casting process.

Engineering challenges

Gripping reliability

  • The initial problem was very broad: reliably picking textiles from flat surfaces.
  • Textiles behave inconsistently due to folds, wrinkles, thickness differences, and edge conditions.
  • The gripper had to work repeatably as part of an automated robotic handling process.
  • Difficult grip cases, such as textile corners and edges, had to be handled reliably.

Robust, repeatable parts

  • The gripper had to become more than a one-off prototype: it needed to be robust and repeatable.
  • The silicone mold process had to be improved to avoid imperfections, bubbles, and inconsistent parts.
  • Small manufacturing details had a large effect on the quality and repeatability of the silicone gripper.

Design process

1
Gather requirements

Defined what the gripper needed to achieve in the real application: picking textile pieces from flat surfaces, holding them securely, and fitting into a robotic handling process.

2
Understand the problem

Tested current solutions and observed where they failed, especially with textile edges, corners, and inconsistent folds.

3
Develop and test concepts

Built and evaluated several gripper concepts to find out which approaches worked best for reliable pickup.

4
Iterate the design

Refined the gripper based on test results, with the focus on keeping it simple, reliable, and suitable for robotic integration.

5
Improve part quality

Improved the mold design and silicone casting process to reduce bubbles and surface imperfections and achieve more consistent prototype parts.

Prototype development

Functional testing

Example test showing a difficult grip case close to the textile corner. These edge and corner cases were especially useful for evaluating grip reliability.

Result

The resulting gripper was integrated into two robotic prototype systems that were installed and tested in a real industrial environment.

The project was especially valuable because it transformed a broad and undefined handling problem into a working gripper through testing, iteration, and adaptation to real-world deployment constraints.
